### The Mistake: Trying To Raise Too Much Money

Did you know most college students raise way too much money in their first round?

They see tech headlines about $10M Series As and think that's normal. They pitch VCs with a slide deck asking for $2M before they've made a single sale. The VCs smile, nod, and never respond.

This mistake costs you 6 months of wasted pitches and zero traction.

This mistake leads to the following negative outcomes:

- **You scare away investors:** Investors hear "$2M" and immediately know you don't understand how early-stage fundraising works. They see ambition, but they also see naivety. Most angels won't touch a college kid asking for that much without proof.

- **You waste months pitching the wrong people:** While you're chasing $2M from VCs who will never fund you, your competitors are raising $50K checks from angels and actually building. By the time you realize VCs don't fund pre-revenue college kids, you're 6 months behind.

- **You lose credibility fast:** Once word spreads that you're "the kid asking for $2M with no traction," investors stop taking your calls. In small startup ecosystems, reputation travels fast. You get one shot to make a good first impression.

---

### The Reason This Happens:

You're following advice meant for founders with traction.

Most fundraising content online is written for post-revenue companies raising Series A or B. They've got customers, revenue, and a team. You don't. But when you read "raise what you need to hit your next milestone," you think that means $2M—because your next milestone is scaling to 1M users.

Here are some even more specific reasons why this mistake happens:

- **You overestimate how much you need:** You think you need $2M to build the MVP, hire a team, and scale. But the truth? Most successful college startups raised $25K-$100K for their first round. That's enough to validate, build V1, and prove the model works.

- **You see big numbers in the news:** TechCrunch writes about the $10M rounds, not the $50K checks. So you think that's normal. But 95% of college founders start with friends, family, and small angel checks—not venture capital.

- **You don't know what angels vs VCs fund:** Angels fund potential and people. VCs fund traction and business models. As a college kid with no revenue, you're an angel investment—not a VC investment. But you're pitching the wrong group.

---

### How To Fix It

Start small and prove the model first.

Instead of asking for $2M to "build and scale," ask for $25K-$100K to validate your idea, build an MVP, and get your first 100 users. Once you have proof it works, raising the next round is 10x easier. Investors bet on progress, not promises.

Instead of trying to raise too much money in the first round, here's what you should do instead:

- **Ask for $25K-$100K from angels:** This is enough to validate your idea, build V1, and prove people want it. Angels are much more likely to bet on you at this stage. Once you have traction, you can raise more later.

- **Build a 6-month runway:** Don't ask for 2 years of funding. Ask for 6 months to hit your next milestone (first 100 customers, first $10K in revenue, product-market fit). Investors fund milestones, not timelines.

- **Target angel investors, not VCs:** Angels invest in people and potential. VCs invest in proven business models. As a college founder with no revenue, you're an angel bet. Pitch the right group.

## Example 2: Twitter Growth (Day 3 Snippet)

### The Offer

**Name:** The B2B SaaS Founder's Twitter Roadmap

**The 5 Mistakes:**
1. Writing for everyone instead of a specific audience
2. Posting sporadically (3 times one week, 0 times the next)
3. Only posting—never engaging with others
4. Giving up after 3 months when you don't see results
5. Copying generic "thought leader" advice that sounds like everyone else

### Day 3 Email (Section 2 Example)

### The Mistake: Only Posting—Never Engaging With Others

Here's the truth: Twitter rewards engagement, not just broadcasting.

Most founders treat Twitter like a blog. They post their thoughts, hit publish, and log off. They never reply to comments. They never quote tweet others. They never join conversations. Then they wonder why nobody engages with their content.

This is like showing up to a networking event, giving a speech, and leaving without talking to anyone.

This mistake leads to the following negative outcomes:

- **Your posts get 0 engagement:** Twitter's algorithm prioritizes accounts that engage. If you only post and never reply, the algorithm assumes you're a bot or spammer. Your reach drops to near zero within weeks.

- **You spend 2 hours/day writing for nothing:** You craft the perfect tweet. You schedule it. You wait. 3 likes. 0 replies. Meanwhile, someone with 100 followers who replies to 20 people per day is growing faster than you.

- **You miss the whole point of Twitter:** Twitter isn't a broadcast platform—it's a conversation platform. The founders who grow fastest don't just post. They reply, quote tweet, and join conversations. That's how you build relationships and get discovered.

## Key Patterns Across Examples

### Offer Structure
- Specific audience (not "people")
- 3 tangible outcomes (numbers, specifics, concrete scenarios)
- Golden word in name
- Whisper at the end

### Email Structure
- Consistent 5 sections
- 1/3/1 rhythm in every prose block
- 3-5 bullets per section (1-3 sentences each)
- Under 1000 words total

### Tangibility
- College example: "6 months behind," "$2M," "first 100 users"
- Twitter example: "0 engagement," "2 hours/day," "3 likes"

Use these examples as templates for your own EECs.
